Most accounts of getting into research seem too easy. Someone lists the lab they joined freshman year, the poster, the paper, and it lines up into a path that looks like it was planned. Mine wasn't, and I don't think the tidy version helps anyone who is actually standing at the start of it with no lab, no connections, and a nagging sense that everyone else got a head start. So here is the unedited version, in case you are on the long way in too.

I started as a neuroscience major who wanted to add a statistics minor. My school at the time, Florida Atlantic, wouldn't let me, and that one administrative "no" is the reason for everything downstream. I transferred to Florida State because it gave me the flexibility to take the quantitative coursework I actually wanted. If you are early and you already know you want to do computational work, I'd weight that flexibility heavily when you choose where to be. The specific lab you imagine joining may not exist wherever you land, but a program that lets you build the stats-and-coding half of your training yourself is worth more than a program that boxes you in.

Before the transfer I did a computational neuroscience summer at Carnegie Mellon, going in with basically no research experience beyond some biostatistics and data-science coursework. It was a hard adjustment. Independent research has its own obstacles that nothing in a problem set prepares you for: there is no answer key, the timeline is vague, and most of the work is finding out that the thing you tried doesn't work yet. I mention it because summer programs are one of the few doors that open for people without a lab of their own. You don't need prior research experience to apply to a lot of them, which is exactly the chicken-and-egg problem they exist to solve. Apply to more of them than feels reasonable.

Then I got to FSU and hit the wall that this whole post is really about. I counted maybe two to four faculty in my field on the entire campus. I emailed them. I tried to join a lab, got handed off to a PhD student, and then got quietly ghosted until it was clear it wasn't happening. This is the part people don't warn you about: getting into research is mostly a coordination and attention problem, not a talent one. Faculty are busy, grad students are overloaded, and a cold email from an undergrad with a thin CV is easy to leave in the inbox. Being ghosted is not a verdict on you. It is the base rate. Send more emails than you think you should, make each one specific to that person's actual work, and don't read silence as a review of your worth.

What I did next is the part I'd least recommend as a general strategy and am also most glad I did. I ran out of patience. During a week the campus was closed for snow, I started an organization, CompNeuroSociety, mostly because I was tired of waiting for someone else to make the thing I needed. There was no mission statement and no plan. There was a gap where a computational neuroscience community should have been, and I decided to stand in it.

The move that made it real was less dramatic than founding anything. Once I'd committed to running meetings, I spent a lot of nights reading textbook chapters and papers so I'd actually have something to stand on. I didn't have research authority, so I built a little of it the only way available, by learning the material well enough to teach it. That turned out to be the actual mechanism. The org wasn't a shortcut around my lack of experience; it was a structure that forced me to close the gap, because now other people were showing up expecting me to know things. If you can't get into a lab, building something that obligates you to learn is a surprisingly good substitute. A reading group, a workshop, a small collaboration, anything with other people counting on you.

I want to be honest about the costs, because the founder framing hides them. I initially wanted a peer from my CMU summer to lead it, someone with more research experience and better social instincts than me, and she ended up not putting in the time it needed. A lot of people will hand you ideas and enthusiasm and then not follow through, and you learn to plan around that. And I made it harder on myself than I had to. Early on I burned bridges reacting to things that were genuinely done wrong to me. Being right about them cost me more than letting them go would have. If I could pass one thing back to my earlier self, it's that your own peace and your community are worth more than winning a specific fight. Take the action that keeps the problem from recurring, and stop there.

I'm wary of tying this into a neat lesson, because the neat version is the same lie I started by complaining about. It's tempting to say the detour made me a better scientist. I don't fully buy that. What I'll actually claim is narrower: the long way in gave me a feel for what research costs when you count it in people and patience instead of in insight, and it got me doing computational neuroscience in flies at Janelia now, by a route that started with an administrator refusing a minor. Two things that shouldn't both be true kind of are at once: it was just a student org I shouldn't take too seriously, and it was the most useful training I've had.

If you take anything from this, let it be that the path not looking like the brochure is not evidence you're failing at it. The transfer, the cold summer, the ghosting, the thing you had to build yourself because nobody built it for you -- that can be the path, not a detour from it. Send the next email. Start the small thing. The clean story gets written afterward, if at all, and it's never where the learning happened.
