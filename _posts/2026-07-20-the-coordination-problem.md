I started CompNeuroSociety during a week campus was closed for snow. There was no mission statement and no plan. I was tired of waiting on someone else to fill a gap.

The backstory is less romantic than "founder." I transferred to FSU for a stats minor my old school wouldn't let me add. I came off a computational neuroscience summer at Carnegie Mellon with almost no research experience going in, and found maybe two to four people in the field on campus. I emailed faculty. I tried to join a lab and it went quiet on me. So during the snow week I made the thing, then spent four hours a day for a week on chapters and papers so I'd have something to stand on at the first meeting.

Running an org taught me things that never show up in a paper. A paper is the finished version. You don't see the part underneath, where much of what moves research forward is dealing with people.

It taught me about motivation and follow-through, which I used to think were the same thing. They aren't. Motivation is cheap, and everyone who shows up is motivated, that's why they showed up. But it fades once the semester gets busy. People hand you real ideas and then don't touch them again unless you get annoying about it. That's not bitterness, it's the base rate. Twelve say they'll come, four do, and the four are never the four you'd have bet on. Because of this you stop planning around the average and start planning around the variance.

Staying calm inside all that was the hard part. Early on I burned bridges over things genuinely done wrong to me, but being right cost me more than letting them go would have. There's a line I keep coming back to, turn the other cheek, and you don't have to be religious to see the math. Your peace and your community are worth more than one fight. Take the action that stops it happening again, and don't become the asshole dictator. (I've been the asshole. It's not worth it.)

The clean ending would be that the org made me a better scientist, and I'm not sure I buy that. Two things are true at once. It's a student org and I shouldn't take it that seriously. It's also the most useful training I've had in what research runs on: coordination much more than intelligence.

What I took is harder to put on a resume. I know what knowledge costs when you count it in people and time instead of insight, which has made me more patient when results come slow and warier of anything too clean.
